Sentence Structure

Building sentences from clauses — and understanding what makes them work.

Although she was tired, she finished the report, and she sent it before midnight. (compound-complex)

The Building Block: The Clause

Every sentence is built from clauses. A clause must have a subject and a verb — but not all clauses are equal.

A clause is a group of words containing a subject (who or what does something) and a verb (the action or state). The key question is: can it stand alone as a complete sentence?
✓ Independent Clause
"The rain stopped."
Complete thought — works as a sentence on its own.
✗ Dependent Clause
"Because the rain stopped."
Incomplete — leaves you waiting for the rest. Needs an independent clause.
💡 Think of an independent clause as a full meal. A dependent clause is a side dish — it adds flavour, but can't be served on its own.

The Four Sentence Types

Every sentence in English is one of these four types — defined by how many independent and dependent clauses it contains.

① Simple
1 independent clause. No dependent clauses.
She laughed.
The old man walked slowly down the road.
② Compound
2+ independent clauses joined by a coordinator or semicolon. No dependent clauses.
She laughed, but he frowned.
I was tired; I went to bed early.
③ Complex
1 independent clause + 1+ dependent clauses.
Although it was late, she kept working.
He left because he was bored.
④ Compound-Complex
2+ independent clauses + 1+ dependent clauses.
When the alarm rang, she got up, and she made coffee.
🔗 Coordinators for compound sentences — remember FANBOYS
For · And · Nor · But · Or · Yet · So
These join two independent clauses with a comma before them: "I was hungry, so I made lunch."
🔑 Quick test: Count the independent clauses (IC) and dependent clauses (DC).
1 IC + 0 DC = Simple  ·  2+ IC + 0 DC = Compound  ·  1 IC + 1+ DC = Complex  ·  2+ IC + 1+ DC = Compound-Complex
