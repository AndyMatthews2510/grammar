The words that hold your ideas together and show how they connect.

"Although the weather was cold, we enjoyed the walk."

"He plays the piano. In addition, he speaks three languages."

What Are Linking Words?

These are words and phrases that connect ideas — within a sentence, between sentences, or across a whole paragraph.

Linking words show the relationship between ideas. They tell your reader whether you are adding, contrasting, explaining, giving a reason, showing a result, giving an example, or summarising. Without them, writing feels like a list of disconnected facts. With them, it flows.
💡 Think of linking words as the signposts in a piece of writing. They tell the reader where the text is going next: "here comes a contrast", "here is an example", "here is the conclusion".

Seven Types of Linking Word

Each group does a different job. The colour-coded key below matches the cards.

Adding Contrasting Reasons Results Examples Sequencing Summing up
Adding information
and · also · too · as well · in addition · furthermore · moreover · besides · what is more
Use to add another point that supports or extends the first. Also goes before the main verb (or after be); too and as well go at the end; in addition / furthermore / moreover start a new sentence.
"She is clever. She is also very kind."
"He plays guitar. He sings, too."
"Furthermore, the study found that sleep improves memory."
Contrasting / showing the opposite
but · however · although · even though · though · while · whereas · despite · in spite of · nevertheless · on the other hand · yet
But joins two clauses. However starts a new sentence (or sits between commas). Although/even though need a full clause after them. Despite/in spite of need a noun or -ing form.
"She was tired, but she kept working."
"Although she was tired, she kept working."
"Despite her tiredness, she kept working."
"It rained. However, we enjoyed the walk."
Giving reasons
because · since · as · due to · owing to · because of · on account of
Because / since / as are followed by a full clause (subject + verb). Because of / due to / owing to are followed by a noun or noun phrase — never a full clause.
"She left early because she was tired."
"She left early because of her tiredness."
"Since the weather was bad, we stayed in."
"Due to bad weather, we stayed in."
Showing results
so · therefore · as a result · consequently · thus · hence · for this reason · this means that
So joins two clauses in one sentence. Therefore / as a result / consequently usually begin a new sentence, followed by a comma.
"She studied hard, so she passed."
"She studied hard. As a result, she passed."
"The road was closed. Therefore, we took a different route."
Giving examples
for example · for instance · such as · like · including · namely
For example / for instance introduce a complete example sentence. Such as / like / including introduce examples within a sentence (no comma needed before like in informal use).
"Many animals hibernate. For example, bears sleep through winter."
"Some fruits, such as mangoes and papayas, grow in tropical climates."
"She speaks several languages, namely French, Arabic, and Thai."
Ordering and sequencing
first · firstly · to begin with · then · next · after that · subsequently · finally · lastly · in the end
Used to show the order of steps, events or points. Usually placed at the start of a sentence or clause, followed by a comma.
"First, preheat the oven to 180°C."
"Then, mix the flour and butter together."
"Finally, leave it to cool before serving."
Summing up
in conclusion · to conclude · to sum up · in short · overall · in brief · all in all · to summarise
Used at the end of a piece of writing to signal a final statement. Always followed by a comma. Avoid overusing in conclusion — overall and all in all are less formal alternatives.
"In conclusion, the evidence strongly supports this theory."
"All in all, it was a productive meeting."
"To sum up, both approaches have their advantages."

The Most Common Mistakes

These pairs are frequently confused. The key is what comes after each one.

although / even though  vs  despite / in spite of
+ full clausealthough / even though: always followed by a subject and verb.
"Although she was tired, she kept going." ✓
+ noun / -ingdespite / in spite of: followed by a noun or -ing form — never a clause on its own.
"Despite her tiredness, she kept going." ✓   "Despite being tired, she kept going." ✓
"Despite she was tired, she kept going." ✗
because  vs  because of / due to
+ full clausebecause / since / as: followed by a subject and verb.
"She left because she was ill." ✓
+ noun / -ingbecause of / due to / owing to: followed by a noun phrase.
"She left because of her illness." ✓
"She left due to she was ill." ✗
however  vs  but
joins two clausesbut: sits between two clauses in one sentence.
"It was cold, but we went out." ✓   (no comma before but is also acceptable)
new sentencehowever: usually starts a new sentence, followed by a comma. It can also sit in the middle of a sentence between commas, but it cannot join two clauses the way but does.
"It was cold. However, we went out." ✓
"It was cold however we went out." ✗
📍 Position of also · too · as well
also = goes before the main verb, or directly after be: "She also plays tennis." / "She is also a teacher."
too / as well = go at the end of a sentence: "She plays tennis, too." / "She plays tennis as well."
in addition / furthermore / moreover = start a new sentence, followed by a comma.
🔑 The quick test: Look at what comes after the linking word. A subject and verb? → use although, because, however, but. A noun or -ing? → use despite, because of, due to.
