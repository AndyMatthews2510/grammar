Grammar Made Simple

The Conditionals

Expressing what happens — or would happen — under different conditions.

If she had studied harder, she would have passed the exam.

What Are Conditionals?

A conditional sentence has two parts: the if-clause (the condition) and the result clause (what happens).

Every conditional has an if-clause stating the condition, and a result clause showing the consequence. The verb forms you choose tell your listener how likely or real the situation is — from always true, to possible, to imaginary, to impossible.
Zero Conditional — Always True
General facts, scientific truths, things that always happen.
If + present simple , present simple
"If you heat water to 100°C, it boils."
First Conditional — Real / Likely Future
Possible future situations that could realistically happen.
If + present simple , will + base verb
"If it rains tomorrow, we will cancel the picnic."
Second Conditional — Unreal Present / Future
Imaginary or unlikely situations — things that are not true now.
If + past simple , would + base verb
"If I won the lottery, I would buy a house." (I probably won't win)
Note: use were for all subjects in formal writing: "If I were you…"
Third Conditional — Unreal Past
Imagining a different outcome in the past — the situation cannot be changed now.
If + past perfect , would have + past participle
"If she had practised more, she would have won the competition."

🔑 Key shortcut: Count back. Zero = both present. First = present + will (or can / might / should). Second = past + would (or could / might). Third = past perfect + would have (or could have / might have). See the next section for how these choices change the meaning.

It's Not Just "Would"

"Would" is the most common choice, but other modals work in the result clause too — and they change the meaning. This is a very common source of confusion.

First Conditional — Alternatives to "will"
will — certain result
"If it rains, we will cancel."
can — permission / ability
"If you finish early, you can leave."
may / might — possibility
"If it rains, we might cancel."
should — advice / recommendation
"If you're tired, you should rest."
must — strong advice / obligation
"If you visit Rome, you must see the Colosseum."
Second Conditional — Alternatives to "would"
would — imagined certain result
"If I won, I would buy a house."
could — imagined ability / option
"If I had wings, I could fly."
might — imagined possibility
"If I had more time, I might learn piano."
Third Conditional — Alternatives to "would have"
would have — imagined certain past result
"If she'd studied, she would have passed."
could have — imagined past ability / option
"If we'd left earlier, we could have caught it."
might have — imagined past possibility
"If he'd studied, he might have passed."
🔑 The difference in a nutshell: will / would / would have = the expected result. can / could / could have = the ability or option. may / might / might have = a possibility, not a certainty. should / must = advice or strong recommendation (first conditional only).
