Grammar Made Simple

Noun Clauses

Using a whole idea as a single word — as the subject, object, or complement.

I know what you did last summer.

What Is a Noun Clause?

It sounds complicated — but you use these all the time without realising it.

A group of words that acts like a single noun inside a sentence. Instead of using a simple word like "it" or "something", you use a whole mini-sentence. It always starts with a connector word like that, what, who, whether, if, how, or a question word.
💡 Think of it as putting an entire thought into a box — and then using that box as a single word in your sentence.
With a simple noun
"I know the answer."
Works — but limited.
With a noun clause
"I know what the answer is."
✓ A whole idea used as a noun!

What Can a Noun Clause Do?

Just like a regular noun, it can play four different roles in a sentence.

Subject
The noun clause is the "doer" — it comes at the start.
What she said surprised everyone.
Object
The noun clause receives the action of the verb.
She told me that she was leaving.
Complement
It comes after be and completes the meaning.
The problem is that nobody listened.
After a preposition
It follows a preposition like about, of, in.
I'm not sure about what you mean.

The Connector Words

The connector word you choose depends on whether you're reporting a statement, a yes/no question, or a question word question.

📢 Reporting statements
that
"She said that she was tired."
(Often "that" can be left out in speech)
❓ Yes / No questions
if · whether
"I don't know if he's coming."
"Ask her whether she wants tea."
🔍 Question words
what · who · where · when · why · how
"Tell me where you found it."
⚠️ Word order warning
Statement order — not question order!
❌ "I know where is he."
✓ "I know where he is."
🔑 Key rule: Inside a noun clause, use statement word order — subject first, then verb. Never flip them like in a question.

Starting a Sentence with "It"

Sometimes a noun clause is really the subject of the sentence — but starting with a long clause sounds heavy. English often moves it to the end and puts a small placeholder word, it, at the front instead.

When a noun clause would make an awkward subject, move it to the end of the sentence and start with it instead. The meaning doesn't change — only the order.
Clause first (heavy)
"That she passed the exam surprised nobody."
Correct, but sounds stiff.
With "it" (natural)
"It surprised nobody that she passed the exam."
✓ Much more natural!
💡 Think of "it" as holding the subject's place at the front of the queue while the real subject — the noun clause — waits at the back.
🔑 Key rule: It + be/verb + (adjective) + that-clause. "That" can often be dropped in speech: "It's clear (that) he's tired."

Giving Strong Advice: the Base-Form Rule

After certain verbs and phrases that give strong advice, orders, or urgent opinions, the verb inside the clause behaves in a special way.

After words like suggest, recommend, insist, demand, propose — or phrases like it's important / essential / vital that — the verb inside the clause stays in its plain base form (no -s, no past tense, no "to"), no matter who or what the subject is.
A normal fact
"I know that he arrives on time."
Regular verb form — matches the subject.
Strong advice
"I suggest that he arrive on time."
✓ Base form — never "arrives" or "arrived".
💡 It doesn't matter if the subject is "he", "she" or "it" — the verb never changes. Even "be" stays in its plain form: "It's essential that she be on time," not "is".
🔑 Key rule: Strong-advice word/phrase + subject + base-form verb. For negatives, put "not" before the base form: "It's essential that she not be late."
