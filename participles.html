Participles & Participial Phrases

Turning verbs into descriptions — and making sentences more vivid.

Exhausted by the heat, the children sat in the shade.

What Is a Participle?

A participle is a verb that has been borrowed to do the job of an adjective — it describes a noun.

A participle is a verb form used as a describing word. Instead of saying what something does, it tells you what something is like. There are two kinds: present participles (ending in -ing) and past participles (usually ending in -ed or an irregular form).
💡 Think of it as a verb that has "changed jobs" — instead of showing an action, it now describes a noun, just like any other adjective would.
⏩ Present Participle
verb + -ing
Describes something actively doing something, or an ongoing state.
"The barking dog kept us awake."
"A rising tide lifts all boats."
⏪ Past Participle
verb + -ed  /  irregular form
Describes something that has had something done to it — often passive.
"A broken promise is hard to forgive."
"The stolen painting was never found."

What Is a Participial Phrase?

When a participle gets company — extra words that go with it — it becomes a participial phrase.

A participial phrase is a participle plus extra words (like an object, or describing words) that all work together as one big description. The whole phrase acts as an adjective, describing a noun in the main sentence.
Just a participle
The sleeping baby is cute.
One word doing the describing.
A participial phrase
Sleeping soundly in her crib, the baby looked peaceful.
✓ Several words working together as one description.

Where Does It Go — and the Comma Rule

A participial phrase must sit close to the noun it describes. Where it sits determines whether you need a comma.

comma ✓
At the start of the sentence
Describes the subject. Always followed by a comma.
Running late, she grabbed her keys and rushed out.
no comma
Directly after the noun it describes
Sits right after its noun and is essential to meaning. No commas.
The man sitting by the window is my teacher.
comma ✓
At the end — adding extra detail
Adds non-essential information. Preceded by a comma.
She arrived at the party, wearing a red dress.
⚠️ Watch Out: The Dangling Participle
❌The participial phrase must describe the subject of the main clause. If it doesn't, the sentence becomes confusing — or funny!
❌"Running down the street, the rain started."  (The rain wasn't running!)
✓"Running down the street, she got soaked by the rain."
🔑 Key rule: The participial phrase describes whoever or whatever comes right after it. Always check that it's pointing at the right noun!
