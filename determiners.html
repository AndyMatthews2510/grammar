Determiners

The words that tell us which one, how many, or whose.

"Each student gets a turn."  ·  "Every seat was taken."
"Neither option worked."  ·  "Both teams played well."
"Do you have any questions?"  ·  "Would you like some tea?"

What Are Determiners?

A determiner goes before a noun to tell us more about it — which one, how many, or whether it is specific or general.

Determiners are words that introduce a noun. You already know some well — articles (a, an, the), possessives (my, your, their), and numbers (one, two, three). This lesson focuses on the ones that cause the most confusion: each, every, all, both, either, neither, some and any.
💡 Think of a determiner as a label you attach to a noun before you hand it to the listener: "I'm giving you information about some apples" is very different from "every apple" or "neither apple".

each · every · all · both

All four refer to the whole of a group — but with very different angles. This is one of the most tested areas in English grammar.

each
Individual members of a group, considered one by one. Can be used with two or more things.
+ singular noun
"Each student received a different question paper."
"She kissed each child goodnight."
every
All members of a group as a whole. Used for three or more. Also used for frequency and generalisations.
+ singular noun
"Every seat in the hall was taken."
"The bus comes every ten minutes."
all
The complete group — nothing left out. Used with both countable (plural) and uncountable nouns.
+ plural or uncountable
"All the students passed."
"She ate all the cake."
"All was quiet."
both
Exactly two things or people, together. Not used with three or more.
+ plural noun (two only)
"Both teams played well."
"She can speak both French and Arabic."
each vs every — the key difference
each — one by one
"Each player was given a number."
"Check each item carefully."
Focus on the individual. Implies looking at them separately. Can be used with just two things.
every — the whole group
"Every player in the league trains hard."
"I enjoy every minute of it."
Focus on the group as a whole. Often used with frequency (every day, every ten minutes). Never with just two.
⚠️ all vs both vs every — number matters
both = exactly two  ·  all or every = three or more (or uncountable)
✓ "Both my parents came."  ·  ✓ "All my friends came."  ·  ✗ "Both my three friends came."
all takes a plural noun · every takes a singular noun: "all the seats" ✓ · "every seat" ✓ · "every seats" ✗

some · any

Both refer to an unspecified quantity — but the choice depends on the type of sentence.

SituationUseExample
Positive statementsome"I need some help with this."
Negative statementany"I don't have any money."
Question (genuine / neutral)any"Do you have any questions?"
Offer (expecting yes)some"Would you like some tea?"
Request (expecting yes)some"Could I have some water, please?"
Conditional / ifany"If you have any problems, call me."
⚠️ The offer trap
Even though it is a question, an offer uses some because you expect the answer to be yes:
✓ "Would you like some coffee?"  ·  ✗ "Would you like any coffee?" (sounds as if you hope they'll say no)

either · neither

Both are used with exactly two options. The difference is positive vs negative.

either  vs  neither
either — one or the other
"Either day works for me."
"You can take either road — both lead to the town."
Used with positive verbs. Means "one or the other (or both) of the two". Also used in negative sentences as "either...or": "You can either stay or go."
neither — not one and not the other
"Neither option is perfect."
"I've tried both restaurants — neither was very good."
Used with positive verbs (the negative meaning is built in). Also used in pairs: "neither...nor": "She speaks neither French nor German."
⚠️ neither + positive verb
Because neither already has a negative meaning, do not add another negative:
✓ "Neither of them came."  ·  ✗ "Neither of them didn't come."
✓ "Neither answer is correct."  ·  ✗ "Neither answer isn't correct."
🔑 Quick reference:
either / neither = two options  ·  any / none = three or more
both = the two together  ·  all = the whole group (3+)
each = one by one  ·  every = all as one group (3+)

this · that · these · those

These four point to a specific noun — either near or far in space or time.

Near — in space or time
this · these
this + singular  ·  these + plural
Close to the speaker, or happening now / recently.
"This book is excellent." (I'm holding it)
"These biscuits are delicious." (on the table here)
"This year has been very busy." (the current year)
Far — in space or time
that · those
that + singular  ·  those + plural
Further away, or in the past / already mentioned.
"That building over there is the museum."
"Those were the best years of my life." (past)
"That is a great idea." (just mentioned)
⚠️ that for previously mentioned ideas
That (singular) is often used to refer back to something just said — even if it is not physically distant:
"She said she was leaving. That surprised everyone." ✓
"He offered to help. This was very kind of him." ✓ (also possible — referring to what just happened)
