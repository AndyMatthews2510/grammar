Countable & Uncountable Nouns

Some nouns can be counted — others cannot. The difference changes everything.

Countable: one idea · two ideas · a suggestion · many suggestions
Uncountable: information · advice · furniture · traffic

Countable vs Uncountable

The simplest test: can you put a number in front of it? Three chairs ✓   Three furniture ✗

✓ Countable Nouns
  • Can be singular or plural
  • Can use a / an before them
  • Can use numbers: one, two, three…
  • Can use many and a few
"I have a question."
"She gave me three suggestions."
"There are many reasons for this."
"I only have a few minutes."
✗ Uncountable Nouns
  • No plural form — always singular
  • Cannot use a / an before them
  • Cannot use numbers directly
  • Use much and a little
❌ "I have an advice."   ✓ "I have some advice."
❌ "She gave me three informations."
✓ "There is a lot of information here."
✓ "I only have a little time."
💡 Think of uncountable nouns as substances or abstract concepts that exist as a mass rather than as individual items — like water, sand or knowledge. You can scoop some out, but you can't count individual pieces.

Quantifiers — Choosing the Right One

Several common words only work with one type of noun. Getting this wrong is one of the most frequent mistakes in written English.

QuantifierUse withExample
manyCountable (plural)How many students are there?
muchUncountableHow much time do we have?
a fewCountable (plural) — some, but not manyI have a few ideas.
fewCountable (plural) — not many, less than expectedVery few people attended.
a littleUncountable — some, but not muchAdd a little salt.
littleUncountable — not much, less than expectedThere is very little hope left.
some / anyBoth (any type)Some people arrived early. Do you have any milk?
a lot of / lots ofBoth (any type)A lot of chairs. A lot of furniture.
enough / plenty ofBoth (any type)Enough time. Enough chairs.
⚠️ a few vs a little — and few vs little
a few / a little = positive — there is some: "I have a little money" = enough for now.
few / little (no article) = negative — there is not enough: "I have little money" = barely any, it's a problem.

Nouns That Can Be Both

Some nouns change their meaning depending on whether they are used as countable or uncountable.

coffee
countable (a serving)"Two coffees, please." (two cups)
uncountable (the substance)"Coffee is grown in Brazil." (the crop/drink in general)
experience
countable (an event)"It was an experience I'll never forget."
uncountable (knowledge/skill)"She has ten years of experience." ❌ an experience
time
countable (an occasion)"How many times have you been there?"
uncountable (the concept)"I don't have time to explain." ❌ a time
work
countable (a creative piece)"She has published several works of fiction."
uncountable (activity/employment)"I have so much work to do." ❌ a work
glass
countable (the object)"She dropped a glass on the floor."
uncountable (the material)"The table is made of glass."

Common Uncountable Nouns — The Tricky Ones

These are frequently used words that learners often mistakenly treat as countable. Learn them as a group.

advice❌ an advice
✓ a piece of advice
information❌ informations
✓ some information
news❌ a news (looks plural!)
✓ "The news is good."
furniture❌ furnitures
✓ a piece of furniture
luggage❌ luggages
✓ a bag / a suitcase
progress❌ a progress
✓ make progress
research❌ a research
✓ a piece of research
knowledge❌ knowledges
✓ knowledge of the topic
traffic❌ traffics
✓ heavy traffic
equipment❌ an equipment
✓ a piece of equipment
homework❌ homeworks
✓ do homework
moneyUse coins, notes, sums to count.
✓ a sum of money

Partitive expressions — how to "count" the uncountable:

a piece of advice / information / furniture / equipment / research / news
a bit of luck / help / fun / trouble / advice
a cup / glass of coffee / tea / water / juice / milk
a bar of chocolate / soap
a loaf / slice of bread
a sheet / piece of paper / work
🔑 Quick tests: Can you say a ___ or two ___s? → Countable. Does it feel like a substance, mass or abstract idea? → Probably uncountable. If unsure, check — many common English nouns surprise even advanced learners.
