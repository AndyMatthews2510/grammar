Adjective Clauses

Describing people, things, and places — in your own words.

The teacher who lives next door won an award.

What Is an Adjective Clause?

A simple idea — it's just a description that uses a whole mini-sentence.

A group of words that describes a noun (a person, place, or thing). It works like a built-in description, and it always starts with a connector word like who, which, that, whose, where, or when.
💡 Think of it as a sticky label attached to a noun — it tells you which one or what kind.
Without the clause
"The man is my uncle."
Which man? We don't know.
With the clause
"The man who is wearing a red hat is my uncle."
✓ Now we know exactly which man!

The Connector Words

The word you choose tells your reader what kind of noun is being described.

who
For people
"The student who sits next to me is from Japan."
which
For things & animals
"The book which I borrowed is overdue."
that
For people or things (informal)
"The film that we watched was amazing."
whose
Shows belonging
"The girl whose bag was stolen called the police."
where
For places
"The café where we met has closed down."
when
For times
"I remember the day when we first met."

Where Does It Go — and the Comma Rule

The clause always sits right after the noun it describes. But commas depend on whether the information is essential.

No commas — defining
Defining clause
The information is essential — without it, we don't know which person/thing is meant.
The woman who called you left a message.  (which woman? the one who called)
Commas — non-defining
Non-defining clause
The information is extra detail — we already know which one. Remove it and the sentence still makes sense.
My sister, who lives in London, is visiting next week.  (I only have one sister — the clause is bonus info)
🔑 Quick test: Remove the clause. If the sentence loses its meaning, no commas. If it still makes sense, add commas.
