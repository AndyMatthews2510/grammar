Grammar Made Simple

Gerunds & Infinitives

Two ways to use a verb as a noun — and when the choice changes the meaning entirely.

Gerund: "She enjoys swimming."  ·  "Smoking is harmful."
Infinitive: "She wants to swim."  ·  "He decided to leave."

What Are Gerunds and Infinitives?

Both allow a verb to act like a noun in a sentence — as the subject, object or complement.

A gerund is a verb with -ing that functions as a noun: "Swimming is relaxing."
An infinitive is to + base verb that also functions as a noun: "To swim is relaxing."

The challenge is knowing which one follows which verbs — and the special cases where the choice changes the meaning entirely.
💡 Think of gerunds and infinitives as two different ways to package an action so it can sit in a noun's position. Most verbs in English have a preference for one or the other — or sometimes both.

Which Verbs Take Which Form?

Most verbs that take a gerund or infinitive do so consistently — these lists are worth learning by heart.

Verbs followed by Gerund only
verb + -ing
enjoy · avoid · finish · mind
suggest · recommend · consider
keep · admit · deny · risk
practise · miss · imagine
can't help · give up · put off
"She enjoys swimming." ✓
"He admitted taking the money." ✓
"They suggested meeting later." ✓
❌ "She enjoys to swim."
Verbs followed by Infinitive only
to + base verb
want · need · hope · decide
plan · promise · agree · refuse
manage · afford · expect · offer
seem · appear · tend · fail
learn · pretend · threaten
"She wants to leave." ✓
"He managed to finish on time." ✓
"They agreed to meet at noon." ✓
❌ "She wants leaving."
Verbs + either form (same meaning)
verb + -ing   or   to + base verb
begin · start · continue
like · love · hate · prefer
intend · attempt · bother
"She started crying." = "She started to cry."
"I like swimming." = "I like to swim."
"He continued working." = "…to work."
Gerund as subject of a sentence
When a gerund comes first in a sentence, it is the grammatical subject — treated as singular.
"Swimming every day is great for your health."
"Smoking is harmful."
"Learning a language takes time."
Note: "To swim is great" is possible but formal; gerunds are more natural as subjects.

Same Verb, Different Meaning

With these verbs, choosing gerund or infinitive changes the meaning significantly. This is the most important — and most tested — area of this topic.

stop
+ gerund
"She stopped smoking."
= She quit the habit permanently. Smoking was the activity she stopped doing.
+ infinitive
"She stopped to smoke."
= She paused what she was doing in order to have a cigarette. She is still a smoker.
remember
+ gerund
"I remember locking the door."
= I have a memory of doing it. The action happened first; the memory is now.
+ infinitive
"Remember to lock the door!"
= Don't forget to do it. The action hasn't happened yet — it needs to happen in the future.
forget
+ gerund
"I'll never forget meeting her."
= I cannot forget the memory of that past event. Meeting happened first.
+ infinitive
"I forgot to meet her."
= I failed to do something I was supposed to do. I didn't meet her — it was an obligation I missed.
try
+ gerund
"Try drinking more water."
= Experiment with it as a possible solution. It might help — give it a go.
+ infinitive
"I'm trying to finish this report."
= Making an effort to achieve something difficult. There is a sense of struggle or challenge.
regret
+ gerund
"I regret saying that."
= I feel sorry about something I said in the past. The action has already happened.
+ infinitive
"I regret to inform you…"
= A formal phrase used when delivering bad news. The regret is about the present action of telling.
go on
+ gerund
"She went on talking for an hour."
= She continued doing the same thing without stopping.
+ infinitive
"She went on to become CEO."
= She did something different next — a new stage or achievement followed.
🔑 The pattern for remember, forget, regret: gerund = past action (it already happened) · infinitive = future action (it still needs to happen or is being communicated now).

After Prepositions — Always Gerund

When a verb follows a preposition, it must be a gerund. No exceptions.

Rule: preposition + gerund (-ing)
Any preposition (at, for, about, in, of, on, without…) must be followed by a gerund, not an infinitive.
"She is good at cooking."  ·  "He's thinking about leaving."
"I'm interested in learning more."  ·  "They left without saying goodbye."
"She's afraid of failing."  ·  "Thank you for helping me."
⚠️ The "to" preposition trap
Sometimes to is a preposition, not the infinitive marker. In these cases, the verb that follows must be a gerund:

"I'm looking forward to see you."  →  "…looking forward to seeing you." ✓
"She's used to work late."  →  "…used to working late." ✓
"He objects to be treated like that."  →  "…objects to being treated…" ✓

Test: if you can replace "to" with "towards" or "about", it is a preposition → use gerund.
