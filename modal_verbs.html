Modal Verbs

The verbs that express ability, permission, obligation, possibility and more.

Ability: "You can do this."  ·  Obligation: "You must try."
Possibility: "It might rain."  ·  Advice: "You should rest."

What Are Modal Verbs?

Modal verbs are a special group of auxiliary (helping) verbs. They work differently from regular verbs.

Modal verbs are used before a base verb to add meaning about ability, permission, obligation, deduction, possibility or advice. The modal changes the attitude of the sentence — how certain, how necessary, or how permitted something is.

Key rules — modals behave differently from other verbs:

  • Always followed by a bare infinitive (no "to"): She can swim ✓   She can to swim ✗
  • No -s in third person singular: He must go ✓   He musts go ✗
  • Negatives: add not directly: cannot / can't, must not / mustn't, should not / shouldn't
  • Questions: invert the modal and subject: Can she drive? Must we leave?
  • No infinitive, -ing or past participle form — use alternatives when needed: be able to, have to, be allowed to
💡 Think of modal verbs as dials on an attitude control panel. The base verb says what happens; the modal tells you how certain, how necessary or how permitted it is.

The Six Functions

Each modal verb can express one or more functions. The same modal can mean different things in different contexts — context is key.

Ability
What someone is able to do — present, past or future.
can
Present ability
present"She can speak three languages."
Ability right now.
requests"Can you help me with this?" (informal)
could
Past ability / polite requests
past ability"When I was young, I could run very fast."
polite request"Could you pass the salt, please?" (more polite than can)
be able to
Ability in all tenses
future"She will be able to join us next week."
after modals"You might be able to find it online." (can't use two modals together)
Permission
Asking for or giving permission — or saying what is allowed.
can / can't
Informal permission
giving"You can borrow my car." (I allow it)
refusing"You can't park here." (it's not allowed)
may / may not
Formal permission
asking"May I come in?" (more formal than can)
refusing"You may not leave until you finish." (formal refusal)
could
Polite requests for permission
polite"Could I take a look at that?" (tentative, polite)
Obligation & Necessity
What is required, necessary or forbidden.
must
Strong obligation (internal)
obligation"You must carry your passport at all times." (rule / law)
mustn't"You mustn't smoke in here." = it is forbidden
have to
Obligation (external)
external rule"I have to wear a uniform at work." (rule from outside)
don't have to"You don't have to come." = not necessary (NOT forbidden)
need to / needn't
Necessity
necessary"We need to leave by 8am."
not necessary"You needn't worry — it's fine." = not necessary
⚠️ mustn't vs don't have to — very different meanings!
mustn't= it is forbidden: "You mustn't tell anyone." (Do not do it!)
don't have to= it is not necessary: "You don't have to come." (You can choose.)
Deduction & Certainty
Expressing how certain you are about something — reasoning from evidence.
must
Certain deduction (positive)
logical conclusion"The lights are off — they must be out." (I'm fairly certain)
past"She must have left already." (must + have + pp)
can't / couldn't
Certain deduction (negative)
impossibility"That can't be right — I checked twice." (I'm sure it's false)
past"She can't have forgotten — she wrote it down."
might / could
Uncertain deduction
possibility"That might be true, but I'm not sure." (perhaps)
past"She might have missed the train."
Possibility & Probability
How likely something is to happen — in the present or future.
will
Near certainty
very likely"It will rain tomorrow." (confident prediction)
should
Expected outcome
expectation"The package should arrive by Thursday." (I expect it will)
may / might / could
Open possibility
may"She may be late." (possible — slightly more formal)
might"It might snow tonight." (possible — less certain than may)
could"That could work." (one of several possibilities)
Advice & Recommendation
Suggesting what someone ought to do — from gentle to strong.
should / shouldn't
General advice
advice"You should see a doctor." (I recommend it)
past regret"I should have studied harder." (I didn't, and I regret it)
ought to
Moral obligation / advice
duty"You ought to apologise." (similar to should, slightly stronger moral tone)
had better
Strong advice / warning
urgent"You had better leave now or you'll miss the train." (implies a consequence — stronger than should)
🔑 The certainty scale (deduction):
must → very certain  |  should → expected  |  may → possible  |  might / could → less certain  |  can't → impossible
